A buffer is a solution that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or
base are added. It is made from a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak
base and its conjugate acid). The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation,
pH = pKa + log([A-]/[HA]), relates the pH of the solution to the pKa of the weak
acid and the ratio of conjugate base to conjugate acid.

Buffering capacity is greatest when the pH is close to the pKa, because the
concentrations of the weak acid and its conjugate base are then comparable and
each can neutralize added base or acid. As a rule of thumb a buffer works well
within about one pH unit of its pKa. When the pH equals the pKa, the ratio of
conjugate base to acid is one and the log term is zero.

Diluting a buffer with water changes the absolute concentrations of acid and
base but not their ratio, so the pH stays nearly constant. Adding strong base
converts weak acid into conjugate base, raising the ratio and the pH only
slightly, while adding strong acid does the reverse. Choosing a buffer for an
experiment therefore means picking a weak acid whose pKa is near the target pH.
